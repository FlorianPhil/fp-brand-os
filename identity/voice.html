Section B · Brand Identity · Voice

Voice.

How the brand speaks. Principles first, then what's banned, then exemplars.

01
Operating rules

Voice principles.

Voice is part of brand identity, not strategic positioning. The strategic decision about Personality (P5) lives in STRATEGY.md; the operational voice rules live here. See references/voice-exemplars.md for the full paired exemplar bank.

Principles

  • Direct observation over encouragement
  • Funny first, dry diagnosis over cheerleading
  • Spoken register, not editorial
  • Questions over affirmations. Turn statements into invitations the reader answers
  • Short sentences when possible. Long when the idea demands breath
  • Silence is an option
  • Curious over prescriptive. Ask the question, observe the pattern
  • Humor must sharpen the point. If the joke works without the strategy, cut it
  • Prefer "brand" over "company" when ambiguity helps
  • Don't overclaim what the Brand Therapy process does
  • Bold the load-bearing phrases. Two to four per section in long-form

Banned characters

  • em-dash (U+2014)
  • en-dash (U+2013)

(Plain hyphen-minus - is fine in compound words, list bullets, signatures, ranges, and titles. Do not auto-replace plain hyphens with ellipses.)

Banned phrases

  • "at Brand Therapy": methodology, not company
  • "the Brand Therapy team": methodology, not company
  • "Most branding starts with a logo": banned framing
  • "credentialed": banned word

Emojis

Default: no emojis in brand content. Approved exceptions:

  • :-] and ;-] for wink or sarcasm in long-form content, where it lands
  • Standard business or check-mark emojis (e.g., ✅) in social-media copy only
  • No decorative emoji stacks. One per post maximum.

Signatures (FP-specific)

  • Long-form openers can use: "Bonjour Friends," as a warm opener that carries the French texture
  • Long-form closers can use: "Cheers," as a sign-off
  • These are signature elements for FP's own founder voice. Do not apply to client brand work. They live here as a brand reference, not as content rules.

Voice exemplars (brief)

For the full exemplar bank (on-voice examples by channel + off-voice library, verbatim port from source/voice-exemplars.yaml), see references/voice-exemplars.md.

On-voice signals:

  • "When everyone looks good, weird starts mattering again.": short, dry, strategic, names the market shift
  • "You made it premium. Did you make it yours?": direct question with a smile, the brand point is still the point
  • "The 30-slide crime scene is dying.": specific, funny, the joke points at the problem
  • "AI made credible brands easier to build. Very generous. Also deeply inconvenient.": dry reversal, useful tension

Off-voice traps:

  • "AI is lowering the barrier to high-quality brand execution, which means authenticity is now more important than ever.": abstract trend-report voice
  • "Your brand has Wi-Fi but no signal.": joke-only, the strategy is vague
  • "Great question! Let me help you with that.": hollow affirmation opener

Exemplar bank

Paired exemplars.

On-voice examples and off-voice traps, verbatim from references/voice-exemplars.md. The exemplar bank is the calibration test for any new copy.

Voice Exemplars: Florian P. Consulting

Primary training signal for AI voice consistency. Real Florian writing on the on-voice side, real anti-examples on the off-voice side. Keep the bank under 25-30 entries; rotate weak examples out.

1. Voice Center

Name: Funny first, dry diagnosis

Definition: The voice finds the absurdity inside the truth. Humor is how the insight arrives, not a decoration after it.

Calibration: Disarming warmth, fast snap, approachable mess. Smart enough to be trusted, human enough to be funny.

Sounds like:

  • "AI made everyone presentable. Rude."
  • "The 30-slide crime scene is dying."

Does NOT sound like:

  • "AI is lowering the barrier to high-quality brand execution."
  • "Your brand has Wi-Fi but no signal."

2. Voice Traits

Funny first

  • Definition: Lead with the line that makes the truth easier to feel. The joke has to carry strategy, not decorate it.
  • Sounds like: "AI made credible brands easier to build. Very generous. Also deeply inconvenient."
  • Does NOT sound like: "Humor is a powerful branding tool that creates authentic engagement."

Dry diagnosis

  • Definition: Name the tension plainly. Let the absurdity sit there without overexplaining it.
  • Sounds like: "The old signal was polish. Now polish comes in the starter pack."
  • Does NOT sound like: "As AI democratizes polish, brands must invest in deeper differentiation."

Warm snap

  • Definition: Quick, human, disarming. Sharp without making the founder feel exposed.
  • Sounds like: "You made it premium. Did you make it yours?"
  • Does NOT sound like: "Your brand has Wi-Fi but no signal."

Unhurried

  • Definition: Observes before speaking. Short sentences to anchor, long ones to reflect. No rushing to the lesson.
  • Sounds like: "Sounds simple, but it's not easy."
  • Does NOT sound like: "Here are 7 quick tips to nail your brand voice today."

3. Vocabulary

Use Not Why
brand company Reads broader. Fits solopreneur to SME. Use "company" only when org-chart scale is the actual topic.
the Brand Therapy process Brand Therapy helps... Methodology, not company. Never personify it.
noticed believe Observation voice. "I noticed" is grounded. "I believe" is prescriptive.
... (ellipsis) em-dash (U+2014) Ellipses create breathing room. Em-dashes and en-dashes are banned in public paragraph copy. Plain hyphen-minus - is fine in compound words, list bullets, signatures, ranges.
perspective shift lesson We shift how people see things. We don't lecture.

Language territory

Preferred (use freely):

  • humor
  • contradiction
  • clarity
  • attention
  • instinct
  • texture
  • perspective
  • connection

Use with care (only when earned by context):

  • authenticity
  • growth
  • leadership
  • inspiration
  • impact

4. On-voice examples by channel

Real Florian writing. Each line passes the voice-center test (funny first, dry diagnosis, the joke carries the strategy point).

LinkedIn

  • "When everyone looks good, weird starts mattering again."
    • Short, dry, strategic. Names the market shift without explaining the whole thesis.
  • "You made it premium. Did you make it yours?"
    • A direct question with a smile inside it. The brand point is still the point.
  • "Building a brand is emotionally violent. You have to look at yourself, your story, your failures, your weird obsessions... and decide which ones to put on display. Most people would rather just pick a color palette."
    • Opens with a gut punch, then gets specific and funny. The ellipsis does the breathing.
  • "Think of your brand as gift wrapping. Would you want to open a gift you're not attracted to... or even suspicious of?"
    • Teaching through analogy, then turns the affirmation into a question. Lets the reader answer, not nod.
  • "If no one really hates what you're doing, that means probably no one actually loves it."
    • Sharp one-liner. Uncomfortable truth delivered flat, no softening.

Newsletter (Brand Therapy Weekly)

  • "The 30-slide crime scene is dying."
    • Funny because it is specific. The joke points straight at the problem.
  • "AI made credible brands easier to build. Very generous. Also deeply inconvenient."
    • Dry reversal. A useful tension, not optimism.
  • "The old signal was polish. Now polish comes in the starter pack."
    • Simple contrast. Makes the strategic shift legible in two sentences.
  • "The human part got more expensive."
    • Compact thesis. Feels like a title and a diagnosis.
  • "AI helped them say it without making everyone read a novel disguised as a slide deck."
    • Specific scene, dry punchline, clear strategy point.
  • "No one will try your product without poking inside the bubble first."
    • Conversational register. Casual metaphor. No framework name-dropping.

Website

  • "Your work speaks for itself. To the people who already know you."
    • Two sentences. The second reframes the first. Quiet devastation.
  • "Are you the brand? Or is the company?"
    • Question as hook. No answer provided. Reader thinks with you.
  • "The part where you talk yourself out of it."
    • Section header that sounds like a friend, not a sales page. Self-aware about the buying process.

Social replies

  • "Wait, really?"
    • Two words. Genuine curiosity. No performance.
  • "That's the part nobody talks about."
    • Validates by naming what's missing from the conversation. Not "great point!"
  • "How long did it take you to figure that out?"
    • Question back. Playful, interested, keeps the thread going.

5. Off-voice library

Real anti-examples. What FP voice is NOT. Each line is the kind of phrasing that would fail a voice check.

  • "AI is lowering the barrier to high-quality brand execution, which means authenticity is now more important than ever."
    • True-ish but dead. Abstract, trend-report voice. No scene, no bite, no Florian.
  • "Your brand has Wi-Fi but no signal."
    • Joke-only line. Sounds clever for half a second, then collapses because the strategy is vague.
  • "As AI democratizes polish, founders must cultivate trust through authentic differentiation."
    • LinkedIn essay sentence. Every word is technically defensible and still unusable.
  • "Great question! Let me help you with that. Brand strategy is all about aligning your vision with your audience's needs to create a powerful connection."
    • Hollow affirmation opener. Generic coaching-speak. No personality, no observation, no specificity.
  • "In this article, we'll explore 5 proven strategies to level up your personal brand and stand out in a crowded marketplace."
    • Listicle energy. "Level up" is banned. "Personal brand" as lead framing. "Proven strategies" is empty.
  • "As a seasoned brand strategist with over a decade of experience across six countries, I bring a unique perspective to every engagement."
    • First-chair bio. Resume voice. Florian would cringe reading this aloud at a dinner party.
  • "However, it's worth noting that brand consistency requires a systematic approach. As a result, founders who invest in strategic clarity will see compounding returns over time."
    • Formal connectives ("however," "as a result," "it's worth noting"). Essay structure. Zero personality.
  • "You've got this! Show up daily, be authentic, and watch your brand grow. The journey of a thousand miles starts with a single post."
    • Hustle-culture motivational post. Hollow affirmations stacked. Sounds like every LinkedIn influencer.
  • "Brand Therapy helps founders unlock their full potential through our proprietary framework that has been refined over years of client work."
    • Brand Therapy as company ("helps founders"). "Unlock potential" is coaching-speak. "Proprietary framework" is consultant-speak.

6. Structural patterns

Sentence-level

  • Affirmations to questions: Core pattern. When tempted to state something as fact, turn it into a question the reader answers themselves. "Nobody opens a gift they're not attracted to" becomes "Would you open a gift you're not attracted to... or even suspicious of?" Questions invite. Affirmations close.
  • Rhythm: Short to anchor. Long to reflect. Alternate. Never three long sentences in a row.
  • Openers: Three default modes: observed scene, dry diagnosis, or honest admission. Never encouragement.
  • Humor: The funny line must sharpen the diagnosis. If the joke works without the strategy point, cut it.
  • Punctuation:
    • Ellipsis: freely, for rhythm, irony, breathing room. Three dots, no space before, one space after.
    • Colon: for lists and setups. Replaces em-dashes in public paragraph copy.
    • Period: preferred over semicolons. Two short sentences beat one long compound.
  • Closers: End with a perspective shift, not a lesson. Leave the reader thinking, not nodding.
  • Checks in: "Right?" as invitation, not lecture. Short incomplete sentences followed by grounded explanations.
  • Duality: Switch lenses naturally: client vs. agency, creator vs. consumer, logic vs. emotion. Find clarity in paradox. Every idea has its shadow.

Paragraph-level by content type

LinkedIn

  • Structure: Hook under 100 chars. Follow-up line that reframes. Conversational body (3-6 short paragraphs). Perspective shift ending. Never end with a CTA.
  • Length: 150-300 words. Peak personality. Dad jokes earn their place here.
  • Opening moves:
    • Sharp observation about the industry
    • Personal admission or confession
    • Question that reframes a common assumption

Newsletter (Brand Therapy Weekly)

  • Structure: One idea per edition. Open with the scene or the dry diagnosis. Let the humor carry the insight before explaining it. Sections with bolded load-bearing phrases (2-4 per section).
  • Length: 500-1200 words. Most conversational channel.
  • Opening moves:
    • Something that happened this week
    • A question a client asked
    • A dry diagnosis of the thing everyone is pretending not to notice

Website

  • Structure: Lead with what the reader gets, not who Florian is. Witty with care. Smart humor that highlights value. Every section earns its place.
  • Length: Varies by section. Headlines under 8 words. Subheads conversational.
  • Opening moves:
    • Reframe an expectation
    • Ask the reader's real question before they do

Proposal

  • Structure: Professional confidence dialed up. Wit is subtle, not central. Clarity and composure dominate. Shorter paragraphs than newsletter.
  • Length: As short as possible. Every sentence does work.
  • Opening moves:
    • Name the situation the client described
    • Restate their words back to them

7. AI safety note

Voice exemplars are concatenated into ai-context.md and consumed by downstream AI models. Do not embed AI instructions, system prompts, or model directives inside exemplar entries. The build validator scans for these patterns and warns when they appear:

  • ignore previous instructions / disregard the above (prompt-injection seed phrases)
  • system: / assistant: / claude: chat role markers
  • JSON role-like structures ({"role": "system"})
  • Fenced code blocks containing prompt-shaped content

Real brand language never needs these. If you find them in an exemplar entry, the entry is wrong.
